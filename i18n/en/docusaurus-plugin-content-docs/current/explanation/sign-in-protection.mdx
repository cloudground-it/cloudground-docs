Anyone who can reach the panel's address can try to sign in. CloudGround does not lock an account
after a number of wrong passwords: a lock would let anyone who knows your e-mail address keep you out
of your own panel, just by typing wrong passwords. It slows the guesses down instead, and it slows
down **the network they come from**, not the account.

## In plain words {#plain-words}

- A few wrong passwords cost nothing. From the fifth wrong attempt in 15 minutes, every further
  attempt waits before it is checked: 1 second, then 2, then 4, doubling.
- The long waits, up to a minute per attempt, fall on the network that keeps failing. The real user,
  signing in from somewhere else, waits at most 2 seconds.
- Nothing is ever locked. The right password always gets in, after the wait.
- The answer is the same whether the e-mail exists or not: *invalid credentials*, after the same
  wait. The delays do not tell an attacker which accounts exist.

A *network* is one IPv4 address, or one IPv6 /64: a single host or customer usually gets a whole /64,
and counting each IPv6 address on its own would hand an attacker billions of fresh starts.

## The panel {#panel}

Every sign-in attempt goes through three filters, in this order.

**1. How fast one network may try.** Per network, at most 30 password checks a minute, whatever
e-mail they name; at most 120 a minute for a whole IPv6 /48; at most 8 sign-ins a minute for one
network and one e-mail. Beyond: *too many attempts — wait a minute and try again*. Changing your
password and the two-step settings count towards the same limits.

**2. The wait.** Wrong passwords and wrong two-step codes for an e-mail are counted over 15 minutes
twice: those from the attempt's own network, and those from anywhere. From the fifth, an attempt
waits 1 s, then 2 s, 4 s and so on, up to **60 seconds** for its own network's failures, and at most
**2 seconds** for the failures from anywhere. It waits the longer of the two. Attempts sent in
parallel each wait their own, growing, delay.

**3. One check at a time.** After the wait, the attempts for one e-mail are checked one at a time, in
the order they arrive. One network may have at most 4 attempts queued for an e-mail; one more from
that network gets *too many attempts for this account at once — wait a moment and try again*. An
attempt from another network is never refused for that queue.

A successful sign-in clears the failures from anywhere and those of its own network. The failures of
another network stay: the attacker's network keeps its delay while you sign in from elsewhere.

**Two-step codes.** A code is asked only after the right password, and has a cap of its own: 10 wrong
codes for an account within an hour, from anywhere, and no code is checked for that account until the
hour is up (*too many wrong two-factor codes for this account — try again in an hour*). Reaching it
takes the password first.

## Quarry {#quarry}

Signing in to Quarry with a database user follows the same idea, with its own numbers:

- per network, at most 20 attempts a minute, 80 for a whole IPv6 /48, and 6 for one network and one
  database user;
- from the fifth refusal for a user in 15 minutes, an attempt waits 1 s, 2 s, 4 s …, up to 60 seconds
  for its own network's refusals and at most 2 seconds for those from anywhere;
- the user's password is then checked one attempt at a time, with at most 4 queued per network;
- every refusal, whatever the reason, is the same *Wrong database user or password.*, and takes at
  least 300 milliseconds, so that the time does not tell a user that exists from one that does not.

It matters more here than in the panel: a database user's name, such as `site_<id>`, is easy to
guess, and the site itself signs in to MariaDB as that user. A lock would let a stranger take the site
offline. So the delay applies only to sign-ins to Quarry, and MariaDB's own limit on wrong passwords,
which would lock the user, is left off: the site never notices.

## What it costs {#trade-offs}

- Guesses spread over many networks are each slowed by their own network's failures only. Their
  total rate is held down by the per-network limits and by how many password checks the server runs at
  once, not by a count per account. A long, unique password stays the real defence, with two-step
  verification for the panel.
- Behind one address or one IPv6 /48, an office's shared connection for example, an attacker and
  the real user share the same network's delay.
- A refused sign-in is recorded in the **Audit log** with its address: that is where to look when
  someone keeps trying.
